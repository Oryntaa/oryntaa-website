The gap between an AI demo and an AI system in production is where most budgets disappear.
The demo is easy; the thing you can trust with real customers and real data is not. Here is
how we think about the line.

## What it can do today

Applied to the right problem, current models are genuinely useful. They are strong at
turning unstructured text into structure, at retrieval over knowledge nobody can query by
hand, at drafting and summarizing, and at automating the repetitive judgment that consumes
skilled hours. When there is a clear definition of "correct" and a way to measure it, these
systems earn their place.

## What it can't do reliably

They do not know what they do not know. Without guardrails they will answer confidently
when they should decline, drift as your data changes, and make errors that are expensive
precisely because they look plausible. AI is not a substitute for a process nobody has
defined — it will automate the confusion faster.

## How to tell the difference

Start from the workflow, not the technology. Find the task where intelligence pays for
itself, write down what "correct" means, and test it against real data before committing to
a build. If you cannot define correct, you cannot evaluate the system — and if you cannot
evaluate it, you cannot trust it in production.

## Build evaluation in from the first commit

The systems that survive contact with real users are the ones shipped with a definition of
correct, a way to measure it, and a cost model you can defend. That is not a final step; it
is the first one. Everything else is easier when you can answer, at any moment, whether the
system is getting better or worse.

Used this way, AI is not a gamble. It is engineering — with the same discipline you would
apply to anything else you put in front of a customer.
